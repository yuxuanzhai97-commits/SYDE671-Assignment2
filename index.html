THE ORAPLEOne image. Many frequency bands.

The central idea

An image is more than its objects. Smooth color changes form its low frequencies; fine edges and texture form its high frequencies. Separating and recombining these components makes it possible to find edges, enhance detail, change perception, and blend images.

Part 1 / Fun with filters

From pixels to edges.

1.1 Convolution from scratch

Two implementations compute true convolution by flipping the kernel and accumulating products: four explicit loops, then two loops with a vectorized local sum. Both use zero padding and match SciPy’s built-in convolution with a same-sized output and zero-filled boundaries, including even-sized derivative kernels.

I ∗ K [y, x] = Σᵢ Σⱼ I[y − i, x − j] K[i, j]

The 9 × 9 box kernel averages 81 pixels. Dₓ = [1, −1] and Dᵧ = Dₓᵀ isolate intensity changes along each axis. Signed derivatives use a diverging display; the colors represent sign, not scene color.

Runtime and boundary behavior

The portrait is reduced with the reused binomial downsampler until its longest side is at most 512 pixels. All three implementations receive the same pixels. Zero extension darkens the box-filter boundary because pixels outside the image contribute zero. The two-loop version removes Python’s per-kernel-element loop, although its NumPy-call overhead can outweigh that benefit for tiny derivative kernels. Timings are one measured run and depend on the machine.

1.2 Finite differences

Threshold = 0.08

The gradient combines horizontal and vertical differences as √(Iₓ² + Iᵧ²). A binary mask keeps magnitudes above 0.08 on the normalized [0, 1] image. This retains strong outlines while reducing weaker grass and sky texture; some faint detail is intentionally discarded.

Explore the threshold tradeoff

A lower threshold keeps faint edges and more texture. A higher threshold suppresses noise but breaks weak contours. The middle setting balances the two rather than treating every texture transition as an object boundary.

1.3 Derivative of Gaussian

σ = 1.5 px

A Gaussian made with a normalized one-dimensional Gaussian kernel suppresses small-scale variation before differentiation. Convolving that Gaussian with Dₓ and Dᵧ gives two DoG filters. Associativity lets a single convolution with each DoG replace the two-stage blur-then-difference computation.

The blurred gradients are smoother and less sensitive to high-frequency texture, at the cost of softer edges and weaker fine details. Part 1.2 uses symmetric boundary extension. The DoG equivalence check uses full zero-extended intermediate convolutions and crops once at the end; a boundary margin is excluded from the displayed DoG edge maps to avoid padding artifacts.

Mandatory extension · Gradient orientation in HSV

Hue encodes the full 0–2π orientation cycle; brightness encodes gradient strength, normalized by the 99th percentile. No built-in angle function is used. The implementation inverts tangent by 24 bisections on [0, π/4], swaps axes for steep gradients, and restores the quadrant from the signs of Iₓ and Iᵧ. Near-zero gradients are dark, where orientation has little meaning.

Part 2.1 / Image sharpening

Bring the detail forward.

Unsharp masking adds a scaled high-frequency residual to an image. It boosts existing detail rather than creating new information. The entire operation is implemented as one convolution using the kernel below.

Isharp = I + α(I − Gσ ∗ I) = [(1 + α)δ − αGσ] ∗ I

Gaussian σ = 2 pixels. The same symmetric boundary convention is used for direct and single-filter forms. Assertions check that both formulations agree. Intensities are clipped only when displayed or exported.

Evaluation: blur a sharp reference, then recover

The sharp evaluation image is used only as a reference for this test. Its blurred copy is resharpened with α = 1; the original sharp reference is not used as a sharpening example.

Resharpening improves local contrast but cannot invert the blur. It cannot restore frequencies that have been strongly attenuated, and increasing α can introduce halos or clipped highlights. Mean squared error measures pixel recovery, not perceived image quality.

Part 2.2 / Hybrid images

Two images. One perception.

Low-pass one image, high-pass the other, then add them. The fine component is easier to see up close; smaller viewing size favors broad structure. Use the viewing-size slider to explore this effect.

H = GσL ∗ A + (B − GσH ∗ B)

Frequency analysis · Ronova + Furina

Two approximate eye centers determine a similarity transform: rotation, uniform scale, and translation. The moving image is warped onto the reference canvas and both are cropped around their shared landmarks to a fully valid rectangle. Eye alignment improves correspondence, but different hair, hats, and silhouettes still generate competing contours.

Choosing the cutoff

The working setting is σL = 6 px and σH = 4 px. For a Gaussian, the half-amplitude frequency is √(2 ln 2)/(2πσ), approximately 0.0312 and 0.0468 cycles/pixel respectively. These are Gaussian transition scales, not hard frequency cutoffs. The smaller-sigma experiment leaves more competing detail; larger sigma suppresses more mid-frequency structure.

Log-magnitude spectra

Spectra use the logarithm of one plus the centered Fourier magnitude to avoid log(0). Each spectrum is display-normalized separately, so compare spatial distribution rather than absolute brightness. Low-pass energy concentrates near the center; the high-pass residual reduces that central concentration.

Mandatory extension · Where should color live?

Color in the low-frequency image preserves broad color regions without adding chromatic fine contours. High-frequency color emphasizes colored edges. Keeping both produces the richest palette, but conflicting colors and edges may make the two sources harder to separate. The controls below expose all three choices for each pair.

Derek + Nutmeg now uses corresponding eye centers to correct scale, rotation, and position. A shared face-centered crop retains the landmarks and excludes empty warped corners. The same general alignment and cropping method is applied to the custom hybrid pairs. Matching eyes improves the grouping, but different facial proportions, silhouettes, and backgrounds can still leave competing contours.

Parts 2.3 & 2.4 / Multiresolution blending

A seam at every scale.

Gaussian and Laplacian stacks separate broad appearance from fine detail without reducing image dimensions. A matching Gaussian stack of the mask gives fine bands a narrow seam and coarse bands a wider transition.

Lᵢ = Gᵢ − Gᵢ₊₁   ·   B = Σᵢ [MᵢLᵢ(A) + (1 − Mᵢ)Lᵢ(B)]

The six Gaussian levels have effective σ = 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 pixels, constructed by incremental Gaussian blurs. The last Gaussian is retained as the coarsest Laplacian residual. Summing all bands reconstructs the original, checked to an absolute tolerance of 10⁻¹². No built-in pyramid functions are used.

2.4 Three color blends

White mask regions select the first input; black selects the second. The Oraple uses a vertical step mask. The first custom blend uses a sinusoidally varying seam, and the second uses a closed, asymmetric face-region mask. All three RGB channels share the same blurred mask at each scale.

Multiresolution blending softens the discontinuity seen in direct compositing. It does not fix semantic or geometric mismatches: the custom face blend still has a visible transition near the hair and neck, and the wavy split combines two different silhouettes. Color improves continuity when source colors agree; grayscale removes chromatic conflict but cannot repair alignment.

The main takeaway

Scale changes what matters.

The most important lesson from these experiments is that image manipulation is a balance between scales. Smoothing removes distracting texture but also weak edges; sharpening enhances surviving detail but cannot undo lost information. Blending succeeds when the mask changes at the right scale for each frequency band. Across all three tasks, the numerical operation alone is not enough: alignment, boundary handling, and display choices strongly affect the visible result.
