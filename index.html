The central idea

The same image can look quite different depending on which details are kept. Low frequencies describe broad changes in brightness and color, while high frequencies contain edges and fine texture. In the experiments below, I separate these components and look at what happens when I change or combine them.

Part 1 / Fun with filters

Finding edges.

1.1 Convolution from scratch

I started by writing convolution in two ways. The first uses four loops: two to move across the image and two to move across the filter. The second keeps the image loops but uses a NumPy sum for each patch. Both flip the filter before applying it. I then compared them with SciPy using the same zero padding and output size.

The 9 × 9 box filter averages 81 neighboring pixels. Dₓ = [1, −1] measures changes across columns, and its transpose Dᵧ measures changes across rows. The derivative images use color to show positive and negative values; these are not the original image colors.

Runtime and boundary behavior

I reduced my portrait to a longest side of at most 512 pixels so the four-loop version would finish in a reasonable time. All three methods use exactly the same reduced image. The box filter darkens the outer border because the padding contributes zeros to the average. For the 9 × 9 filter, the two-loop version is much faster than four loops, and SciPy is faster still. With the tiny derivative filters, the extra NumPy calls make two loops slower than four. These timings come from one run, so they may change on another computer.

1.2 Finite difference operator

Threshold = 0.08

I applied the difference filters to the cameraman image and combined the results using √(Iₓ² + Iᵧ²). I used a threshold of 0.08 on the normalized image to turn the magnitude into a binary edge map. This keeps the stronger outlines while removing some of the weaker grass and sky texture, although a few faint edges are lost too.

Explore the threshold tradeoff

At 0.04, more weak edges survive, but so does a lot of texture. At 0.12, the result is cleaner, but weaker contours start to disappear. I kept 0.08 as a compromise between the two.

1.3 Derivative of Gaussian filter

σ = 1.5 px

The raw difference filters pick up a lot of small variations. To reduce this, I first smoothed the image with a Gaussian at σ = 1.5 pixels. I also convolved the Gaussian with each difference filter to make the two DoG filters shown below. This combines smoothing and differentiation into a single filter.

Compared with the raw differences, the smoothed gradients contain less fine texture and the main outlines are easier to follow. The tradeoff is that edges become softer and some small details disappear. For the numerical comparison, I kept the full intermediate convolution and cropped only at the end. I also excluded a narrow boundary margin from the displayed DoG edges so zero padding would not create a false outline around the picture. Part 1.2 uses symmetric padding instead.

Gradient orientation in HSV

For orientation, I used hue to show the gradient direction and brightness to show its strength. To avoid built-in angle functions, I estimated the angle with 24 bisection steps on [0, π/4], using the ratio of the gradient components. I then corrected for steep gradients and the signs of the components to recover the full 0–2π range. Brightness is scaled by the 99th percentile of gradient magnitude. Flat areas stay dark because their direction is not useful.

Part 2.1 / Image sharpening

Sharpening images.

For sharpening, I blurred the image and subtracted that blur from the original to isolate fine detail. Adding some of this detail back increases edge contrast. I combined these steps into a single sharpening filter.

I kept σ = 2 pixels and compared α = 0.5, 1, and 2. The direct expression and single-filter version are checked against each other using the same symmetric padding. Values are clipped only for display and export.

Evaluation: blur a sharp reference, then recover

I kept the sharp evaluation image separate from the sharpening examples. Here, I deliberately blur it, sharpen the blurred copy with α = 1, and compare that result with the original sharp reference.

The recovered image has stronger local contrast than the blurred copy, and its pixel error is lower in this test. It still does not match the original: sharpening can boost detail that remains, but cannot bring back information lost to blur. Turning the amount up further can also produce halos or clipped highlights. The MSE values help compare recovery, but do not tell the whole story about how sharp an image looks.

Part 2.2 / Hybrid images

Making hybrid images.

To make each hybrid, I kept the low frequencies of one image and added the high frequencies of the other. Fine details are easier to see at a large viewing size; shrinking the result makes the broad structure more noticeable. Try the slider under each pair to compare the two views.

Frequency analysis: Ronova + Furina

For Ronova and Furina, I used two corresponding eye centers to line up the faces. These points determine the rotation, scale, and position of one image relative to the other. I then cropped around the shared landmarks, keeping only pixels that are valid in both images. The eyes line up better, but the different hair and headwear still leave overlapping outlines.

Choosing the cutoff

I used σL = 6 pixels for the low-pass image and σH = 4 pixels for the high-pass image. The comparison below also shows smaller and larger values. Smaller values leave more competing detail, while larger values remove more of the middle-frequency structure. For reference, the Gaussian half-amplitude frequency is √(2 ln 2)/(2πσ), giving about 0.0312 and 0.0468 cycles per pixel for the chosen settings. The filters have gradual transitions rather than hard cutoffs.

Log-magnitude spectra

These images show the logarithm of one plus the centered Fourier magnitude. Adding one avoids taking the logarithm of zero. The low-pass result is concentrated near the center, while the high-pass result has less of that central concentration. Each spectrum is normalized separately for display, so its pattern is more meaningful here than its overall brightness.

Comparing color choices

I compared grayscale with three color choices: color in the low-frequency image, color in the high-frequency image, and color in both. Low-frequency color keeps the broad color regions without adding colored fine edges. High-frequency color makes those edges more noticeable. Keeping both gives more color, but can also make the two sources compete. The buttons below make it easier to compare these choices on the same pair.

Parts 2.3 & 2.4 / Multiresolution blending

Blending across scales.

A single blend boundary can look too abrupt. I used Gaussian and Laplacian stacks to blend different scales separately, keeping every level at the original image size. Blurring the mask at each level gives fine details a narrow transition and broad color changes a wider one.

I used six Gaussian levels, with effective σ values of 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 pixels. Subtracting neighboring levels gives the Laplacian bands. I kept the last Gaussian as the residual so adding all the bands reconstructs the original image; the reconstruction is checked with an absolute tolerance of 10⁻¹². The stacks are built without built-in pyramid functions.

2.3 The Oraple stacks

Masked bands and reconstruction

Each row shows a different part of the blend. The first three rows use level 0, level 2, and the coarsest residual: masked apple on the left, masked orange in the middle, and their sum on the right. The last row adds all the bands back together. The RGB detail bands are centered around gray so negative values can be shown.

2.4 Three color blends

The Oraple starts with a straight vertical mask. For Ronova and Furina, I used a wavy seam. For Sparkle and Sparxie, I used a closed, irregular region around the face. White selects the first image and black selects the second. The same mask is applied to all three color channels, then blurred differently at each scale.

The multiresolution result softens the hard boundary visible in direct compositing. It does not hide every mismatch, though. In the face-region blend, the transition is still visible around the hair and neck, while the wavy blend brings together different silhouettes. Color helps when the sources have similar tones, but can make disagreements more obvious. The grayscale comparison removes the color differences without changing the alignment.

What I learned

What made the biggest difference?

My main takeaway is that the filter settings are only part of the result. Blurring makes edge detection cleaner, but removes small details; sharpening increases contrast, but cannot fully undo that loss. With hybrids and blending, getting the images aligned matters just as much as choosing the filters. Separating the image into scales helped me understand why a seam can look smooth in one region and still be obvious around a face or an outline.
